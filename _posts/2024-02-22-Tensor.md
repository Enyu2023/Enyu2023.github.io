Of course, every one-line proof comes with some preparation. Here the preparation is to replace a matrix by its action on the top exterior power of a vector space. That space is one-dimensional, so composition becomes multiplication of scalars.

The proof will be

$$
\bigwedge\nolimits^n(AB)
=
(\bigwedge\nolimits^n A)(\bigwedge\nolimits^n B)
=
(\det A)(\det B)\,\mathrm{id}.
$$

Let us explain what this means, starting with the invariants and coinvariants that motivate the tensor constructions.

## 1. Invariants and coinvariants

Let $$G$$ be a finite group and $$M$$ a representation of $$G$$ over a field $$\mathbb k$$. Its space of invariants is

$$
M^G=\{m\in M:gm=m\text{ for every }g\in G\}.
$$

It is the largest subrepresentation on which $$G$$ acts trivially. There is a corresponding quotient, the space of **coinvariants**:

$$
M_G=M/\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb k}\{gm-m:g\in G,\ m\in M\}.
$$

In the quotient, $$gm$$ and $$m$$ are identified. Thus $$M_G$$ is the largest quotient representation with trivial action. More precisely, a linear map from $$M$$ to a trivial representation is $$G$$-equivariant exactly when it factors through $$M_G$$. This is the quotient counterpart of the inclusion $$M^G\hookrightarrow M$$.

If $$\operatorname{char}\mathbb k$$ does not divide $$\vert G\vert$$, averaging gives the operator

$$
R(m)=\frac{1}{\vert G\vert}\sum_{g\in G}gm.
$$

For every $$h\in G$$, reindexing the sum shows that $$hR(m)=R(m)$$. Also $$R(m)=m$$ for $$m\in M^G$$. Hence $$R$$ is a projection onto $$M^G$$.

It kills every relation $$gm-m$$, so it induces a map $$\overline R:M_G\to M^G$$. On the other hand,

$$
m-R(m)=\frac{1}{\vert G\vert}\sum_{g\in G}(m-gm)
$$

vanishes in $$M_G$$. Therefore the natural map $$M^G\to M_G$$ and $$\overline R$$ are inverse isomorphisms.

The characteristic assumption belongs to this averaging argument. The definitions of invariants and coinvariants themselves make sense in every characteristic.

## 2. Tensor products and permutations

The tensor product $$V\otimes W$$ turns bilinear maps out of $$V\times W$$ into linear maps out of a vector space. Its elementary tensors satisfy

$$
(av+bv')\otimes w
=a(v\otimes w)+b(v'\otimes w),
$$

and the analogous relation in the second factor. These elementary tensors span the tensor product.

A linear map $$A:V\to V$$ consequently induces

$$
A^{\otimes r}:V^{\otimes r}\longrightarrow V^{\otimes r},
\qquad
v_1\otimes\cdots\otimes v_r
\longmapsto
Av_1\otimes\cdots\otimes Av_r.
$$

The symmetric group $$S_r$$ acts by permuting the factors. This action commutes with $$A^{\otimes r}$$, because applying the same map in every factor does not depend on the order of the factors.

Taking coinvariants for the permutation action gives the symmetric power $$\operatorname{Sym}^r V$$. To build determinants, however, we want alternating rather than symmetric expressions.

## 3. Exterior powers

The exterior algebra is the quotient of the tensor algebra

$$
T(V)=\bigoplus_{r\geq 0}V^{\otimes r}
$$

by the two-sided ideal generated by all $$v\otimes v$$. Its degree-$$r$$ part is $$\bigwedge^r V$$, and the image of an elementary tensor is written

$$
v_1\wedge\cdots\wedge v_r.
$$

The relation $$v\wedge v=0$$ implies

$$
v\wedge w=-w\wedge v,
$$

by expanding $$(v+w)\wedge(v+w)=0$$. This quotient definition works over **every field**, including characteristic two.

When $$r!$$ is invertible in $$\mathbb k$$, the signed averaging operator

$$
\operatorname{Alt}
=\frac{1}{r!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_r}
\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\,\sigma
$$

identifies $$\bigwedge^r V$$ with the alternating tensors in $$V^{\otimes r}$$. This is the connection with the averaging discussion above. We do not need that identification for the determinant argument, so there will be no restriction on the characteristic.

Suppose now that $$\dim V=n$$ and $$e_1,\ldots,e_n$$ is a basis. By multilinearity, every wedge is a sum of wedges of basis vectors. Repeated indices give zero, and swapping indices changes the sign. Thus

$$
e_{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{i_r},
\qquad 1\leq i_1<\cdots<i_r\leq n,
$$

span $$\bigwedge^r V$$. They are independent: for each increasing index set, the alternating coordinate functional given by the corresponding minor takes value one on that basis wedge and zero on the others. These functionals descend from the tensor product because they vanish whenever two arguments coincide.

In particular,

$$
\bigwedge\nolimits^n V
=\mathbb k(e_1\wedge\cdots\wedge e_n)
$$

is one-dimensional.

## 4. The determinant is a scalar action

Because a linear map takes equal vectors to equal vectors, $$A^{\otimes r}$$ respects the exterior relations. It therefore induces

$$
\bigwedge\nolimits^r A:
v_1\wedge\cdots\wedge v_r
\longmapsto
Av_1\wedge\cdots\wedge Av_r.
$$

On the one-dimensional space $$\bigwedge^n V$$, this map is multiplication by a scalar. Define that scalar to be $$\det A$$:

$$
Ae_1\wedge\cdots\wedge Ae_n
=(\det A)e_1\wedge\cdots\wedge e_n.
$$

This agrees with the usual determinant. If $$Ae_j=\sum_i a_{ij}e_i$$, expanding the left-hand side and discarding repeated basis vectors gives

$$
\det A=\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)
\prod_{j=1}^n a_{\sigma(j),j}.
$$

For instance, in dimension two,

$$
(ae_1+ce_2)\wedge(be_1+de_2)
=(ad-bc)e_1\wedge e_2.
$$

The definition is independent of the chosen basis: an endomorphism of a one-dimensional vector space acts by the same scalar in every basis.

## 5. The one-line proof

Exterior powers preserve composition. Indeed, on a spanning decomposable wedge, both $$\bigwedge^n(AB)$$ and $$(\bigwedge^n A)\circ(\bigwedge^n B)$$ send

$$
v_1\wedge\cdots\wedge v_n
\quad\text{to}\quad
ABv_1\wedge\cdots\wedge ABv_n.
$$

Therefore, on $$\bigwedge^n V$$,

$$
(\det(AB))\,\mathrm{id}
=\bigwedge\nolimits^n(AB)
=(\bigwedge\nolimits^n A)(\bigwedge\nolimits^n B)
=(\det A)(\det B)\,\mathrm{id}.
$$

Since this space is nonzero and one-dimensional,

$$
\boxed{\det(AB)=\det A\det B.}
$$

Neither matrix needs to be invertible. If one has dependent columns, its top exterior power is simply the zero map.

Over the real numbers, the determinant records what a linear transformation does to an oriented volume element. Applying two transformations in succession multiplies their two scalar effects.
