Every continuous function $$f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$$ is a quotient map onto its image. There is a useful generalization: the domain can be any path-connected space, and the codomain can be any linearly ordered set with its order topology.

The proof uses two simple facts. A path has compact image, and a connected subset of an ordered space contains every point between any two of its points. Together, they let us check closedness one interval at a time, without using sequences or assuming first countability.

## The statement

**Theorem.** Let $$X$$ be a path-connected space and let $$Y$$ be a linearly ordered set equipped with its order topology. Every continuous map

$$
f:X\longrightarrow Y
$$

is a quotient map when viewed as a surjection $$X\to f(X)$$, where $$f(X)$$ carries the subspace topology inherited from $$Y$$.

Recall that a continuous surjection $$q:X\to Z$$ is a **quotient map** if a subset $$A\subseteq Z$$ is closed whenever $$q^{-1}(A)$$ is closed. Continuity already gives the reverse implication.

Equivalently, it is enough to check that $$q(B)$$ is closed for every closed **saturated** subset $$B\subseteq X$$. Here saturation means

$$
B=q^{-1}(q(B)):
$$

if $$B$$ contains one point of a fiber, it contains the entire fiber.

## Proof: use a compact path over each interval

We may assume that $$f$$ is surjective. Indeed, $$f(X)$$ is connected, so it is a convex subset of $$Y$$: if two points of $$f(X)$$ lie on opposite sides of a missing point, the two open rays at that point would separate $$f(X)$$. On a convex subset, the induced order topology agrees with the subspace topology. We can therefore replace $$Y$$ by $$f(X)$$.

An empty image or a one-point image is immediate, so suppose that $$Y$$ has at least two points. Let $$B\subseteq X$$ be closed and saturated. We will show that $$f(B)$$ is closed.

### 1. Closedness on a closed interval

Fix $$a<b$$ in $$Y$$ and choose $$x_a,x_b\in X$$ with

$$
f(x_a)=a,\qquad f(x_b)=b.
$$

Since $$X$$ is path-connected, there is a continuous path $$\gamma:[0,1]\to X$$ joining these points. Put

$$
P=\gamma([0,1]).
$$

The set $$P$$ is compact and connected. Thus $$f(P)$$ is connected and contains both $$a$$ and $$b$$, so

$$
[a,b]\subseteq f(P).
$$

Saturation is the key to the next identity:

$$
f(P)\cap f(B)=f(P\cap B).
$$

For the nontrivial inclusion, take a point of $$f(P)\cap f(B)$$ and choose a preimage in $$P$$. Its fiber meets $$B$$, so saturation forces that preimage to belong to $$B$$ as well.

Now $$P\cap B$$ is a closed subset of the compact space $$P$$, hence compact. Consequently $$f(P\cap B)$$ is compact, and it is closed in $$Y$$ because every linearly ordered space with the order topology is Hausdorff. It follows that

$$
[a,b]\cap f(B)
=[a,b]\cap f(P\cap B)
$$

is closed in $$Y$$, and in particular in $$[a,b]$$.

### 2. Pass from intervals to the whole space

Every point of $$Y$$ has an open neighborhood contained in one of these closed intervals. If the point is neither a minimum nor a maximum, choose $$a<y<b$$ and use $$(a,b)\subseteq[a,b]$$. At a minimum $$m$$, use $$[m,b)\subseteq[m,b]$$; at a maximum $$M$$, use $$(a,M]\subseteq[a,M]$$.

For each such neighborhood $$U$$, the first step shows that $$U\cap f(B)$$ is closed in $$U$$. Closedness is local: these neighborhoods cover $$Y$$, and the relative complements $$U\setminus f(B)$$ are open in $$Y$$. Their union is $$Y\setminus f(B)$$.

Therefore $$f(B)$$ is closed, proving that $$f$$ is a quotient map. $$\square$$

## Why connectedness alone is not enough

The familiar $$K$$-topology gives a counterexample. Set

$$
K=\{1/n:n=1,2,3,\ldots\},
$$

and let $$\mathbb R_K$$ denote the real line with the topology generated by the usual open intervals and the sets $$(a,b)\setminus K$$. This topology is strictly finer than the usual topology: for example, $$\mathbb R\setminus K$$ is open in $$\mathbb R_K$$ but is not a usual open set, since it contains $$0$$.

Nevertheless, $$\mathbb R_K$$ is connected. Away from $$0$$, its topology agrees locally with the usual topology. If $$U,V$$ formed a separation with $$0\in U$$, then $$U$$ would contain $$(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\setminus K$$ for some $$\varepsilon>0$$. No point of $$K\cap(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)$$ could lie in $$V$$: a neighborhood of that point in $$V$$ would contain a usual interval, which would meet $$U$$. Thus $$(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\subseteq U$$, making both $$U$$ and $$V$$ usual open sets and contradicting the connectedness of the usual real line.

The identity map

$$
\operatorname{id}:\mathbb R_K\longrightarrow\mathbb R
$$

is continuous and surjective, but it is not a quotient map. The inverse image of $$\mathbb R\setminus K$$ is open in the domain while that set is not open in the codomain. Thus we really need more than connectedness in the theorem.

## A consequence for finer topologies

Let $$S$$ be a linearly ordered set, let $$\tau_{\mathrm{ord}}$$ be its order topology, and let $$\tau$$ be any strictly finer topology. Then $$(S,\tau)$$ cannot be path-connected.

Otherwise the continuous identity map

$$
\operatorname{id}:(S,\tau)\longrightarrow(S,\tau_{\mathrm{ord}})
$$

would be a quotient map by the theorem. For an identity map, the quotient condition forces the two topologies to agree, contradicting strict fineness. In particular, this also explains why $$\mathbb R_K$$ is not path-connected.
